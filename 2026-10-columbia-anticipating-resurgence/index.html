[~0.5 min]

Measles has resurged three times in eleven years

Weekly US measles cases, 2006–2025, show three major outbreaks: 2014, 2018–19 and 2025. Drake & Rohani, Future Microbiol 2026.

Measles was declared eliminated from the United States in 2000. This is what the weekly case series has looked like since 2006. There were eleven outbreaks by our definition, and three big ones: 2014, the New York outbreak in 2018 and 2019, and last year’s outbreak, the largest since elimination.

I want to leave you holding one question for the next half hour. Could we have seen these coming? I’ll give you my answer near the end, and it isn’t the one I expected when we started.

[~1.5 min]

Resurgence looks sudden because the threshold is crossed before the cases arrive

In a simulation matched to California’s 2006 pertussis resurgence, the tipping point (R0 = 1) comes roughly a year before cases take off. O’Regan & Drake, Theor Ecol 2013.

Here’s why this is hard. Pertussis in California simmered at very low levels for years and then came back in 2006. When we simulate a slowly rising R-naught, the model reproduces the data and puts the tipping point about a year before the resurgence. Before that point you see stuttering chains of sporadic cases, and nothing in the level of incidence tells you that the system has changed.

So if there’s a warning, it won’t be in the counts themselves. It has to be in how the counts fluctuate. The goal of this work is to develop statistics that anticipate the tipping point without committing to any particular transmission model, and that can be computed on a rolling basis as each week’s surveillance report comes in.

[~1.5 min]

Early warning belongs in the watch and warning phases of epidemic intelligence

Epidemic intelligence has three phases (watch, warning and emergency), and each calls for different data, questions and tools. After Han & Drake, EMBO Rep 2016.

Let me place this work in a bigger picture. Barbara Han and I borrowed an idea from severe-weather forecasting: think of outbreak preparedness as watch, warning and emergency. During COVID, most of the modeling energy went into the emergency phase, meaning forecasts of cases, hospitalizations and deaths. The watch and warning phases have their own questions and need their own tools. Spillover risk maps belong to watch. Today’s subject, statistics computed from routine surveillance that might tell us transmission is creeping toward the threshold, belongs to warning.

[~0.5 min]

Why the threshold is detectable

Let me start with why a population drifting toward R equals one should leave any mark in its case counts at all.

A system losing resilience recovers from shocks more slowly

A ball in a shallow basin returns slowly after a push, so its trajectory becomes more variable and more autocorrelated. Scheffer et al., Science 2012.

The core idea is older than epidemiology. Picture the state of the system as a ball in a basin. In a deep basin, a push is corrected quickly. As the basin flattens, the same push takes longer to die away. Under continuous small pushes, that slow recovery shows up two ways: the state wanders further, so variance goes up, and today looks more like yesterday, so autocorrelation goes up. That’s critical slowing down.

[~1.5 min]

For measles, herd immunity leaves almost no margin

R_e = R_0 \, \frac{S}{N} \qquad\qquad p_c = \frac{1 - 1/R_0}{E}

R_0 basic reproduction number: secondary cases from one infection in a wholly susceptible population
R_e effective reproduction number in the current population
S/N fraction of the population susceptible
p_c critical vaccination coverage
E vaccine efficacy against infection

With R_0 ≈ 15 and vaccine efficacy E ≈ 0.95, the critical coverage is about 93%.

The epidemic threshold is where the effective reproduction number equals one. In a homogeneous population, R-e is R-naught times the susceptible fraction, and with an imperfect vaccine the critical coverage is one minus one over R-naught, divided by efficacy. For measles that’s about 93 percent. US kindergarten two-dose coverage was around 93 percent in 2023. So the national average is sitting right at the line, and the averages hide pockets well below it.

[~1 min]

Declining vaccination is a slow driver acting on fast transmission

\dot S = \mu(1-p) - \beta S I - \eta S - \mu S \qquad \dot I = \beta S I + \eta S - (\gamma + \mu) I

\dot p = \epsilon f(S, I, R), \qquad 0 < \epsilon \ll 1

Here’s a simple model, just complex enough to show all the phenomena we care about. It’s an SIR model with births, deaths, importation at rate eta, and vaccination at birth with coverage p. The key move is that p changes slowly, much more slowly than the transmission dynamics. Epsilon is small. That’s a slow-fast system, and it’s a fair caricature of coverage eroding over years while measles moves on the timescale of weeks.

[~1 min]

Stochastic transmission is described exactly by a master equation

\frac{dP(\sigma, t)}{dt} = \sum_{\tilde\sigma \ne \sigma} T(\sigma \mid \tilde\sigma)\, P(\tilde\sigma, t) - \sum_{\tilde\sigma \ne \sigma} T(\tilde\sigma \mid \sigma)\, P(\sigma, t)

Transitions from \sigma = (S, I) go to (S{+}1, I{-}1), (S{-}1, I), (S{+}1, I), (S, I{-}1) or (S, I{+}1).

O’Regan & Drake, Theor Ecol 2013.

Real epidemics are stochastic, so the right object is the probability of being in each state. The master equation tracks probability flowing in and out of each state through births, deaths, infection, recovery and importation. It’s exact, and because it’s nonlinear we can’t solve it.

[~1 min]

The van Kampen expansion turns the fluctuations into a linear Fokker–Planck equation

S = N\phi_S + \sqrt{N}\,\xi_S, \qquad I = N\phi_I + \sqrt{N}\,\xi_I

\frac{\partial \Pi(\xi, t)}{\partial t} = -\sum_{i,j} A_{ij} \frac{\partial (\xi_j \Pi)}{\partial \xi_i} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j} B_{ij} \frac{\partial^2 \Pi}{\partial \xi_i \partial \xi_j}

The mean field gives the deterministic model; the fluctuations are Gaussian, with covariance set by the Jacobian A.

System-size expansion from statistical physics: van Kampen, Adv Chem Phys 1976.

The trick is the system-size expansion, which Nico van Kampen developed in statistical physics and set out in its general form in the 1970s, long before anyone applied it to epidemics. Write each state as a deterministic part of order N plus fluctuations of order root N. At leading order you recover the differential equations from the previous slide. At the next order you get a linear Fokker–Planck equation for the fluctuations. Its solution is a bivariate normal, and everything we want (variance, autocorrelation, power spectrum) is determined by the Jacobian A, which is the stability of the deterministic system.

That’s the one derivation step I’ll show. The rest is in the backup slides.

[~1.5 min]

At R_0 = 1, return time, variance and autocorrelation all diverge

\Lambda = \beta - (\gamma + \mu) \;\to\; 0 \quad \text{as} \quad R_0 \to 1

  • Characteristic return time \tau \approx -1/\Lambda diverges (a linear approximation, valid only near the critical point)
  • Variance diverges
  • Lag-one autocorrelation goes to one

O’Regan & Drake 2013; Drake et al., PLOS Comput Biol 2019.

Here’s the payoff. The leading eigenvalue of that Jacobian goes to zero at the threshold. Close to the critical point, the return time is approximately minus one over the eigenvalue, so it diverges. That’s a linearization, and it only holds in the vicinity of the threshold; far from it, the return time depends on the full nonlinear dynamics. Because the fluctuations are governed by the same eigenvalue, variance diverges and the autocorrelation goes to one. Let me show you what that looks like geometrically.

[~1 min]

Near the critical immunization level, the potential well goes flat

The potential function of the SIR model with vaccination at immunization levels from 90% to 100%; the well is flattest at the critical level (about 94%), where Re = 1. Drake et al., PLOS Comput Biol 2019.

This is the ball in the basin again, but now the basin is computed from the SIR model with vaccination rather than drawn. The mathematical object is called a potential function. Think of it as a landscape. The state of the system, here prevalence, is the ball. The ball rolls downhill toward the bottom of the well, which is the equilibrium, and the steepness of the walls tells you how hard the system is pulled back after a disturbance.

Each white curve is the potential at a different immunization level, from 90 percent at the front to 100 percent at the back. The yellow line traces the bottom of the well, the equilibrium prevalence, which slides toward zero as immunization rises. At 90 percent, in pink, the ball overshoots and rolls back and forth with a period of about a year. Those are damped oscillations, the familiar epidemic cycles of a childhood infection. At high immunization, in blue, the walls are steep, and a disturbance dies away within weeks without oscillating. In between, at the critical immunization level of about 94 percent, where R-effective equals one, the well is almost flat. You can see it in the inset at the top right. A flat well means almost no restoring force, so a disturbance takes a very long time to disappear.

That’s critical slowing down. It’s why variance and autocorrelation rise, and it’s what we’ll be looking for in the data.

[~2 min]

Near the threshold, the indicators have simple closed forms

Indicator Formula
Mean \mu_1 = \dfrac{\zeta/\gamma}{1 - R_0}
Variance \sigma^2 = \dfrac{\zeta/\gamma}{(1 - R_0)^2}
Index of dispersion \sigma^2/\mu_1 = \dfrac{1}{1 - R_0}
Coefficient of variation \sigma/\mu_1 = (\zeta/\gamma)^{-1/2}
Autocorrelation AC(\tau) = \exp\!\big(-(1 - R_0)\gamma\tau\big)

Birth–death–immigration process with sparking rate ζ. Brett, Drake & Rohani, J R Soc Interface 2017.

For the simplest discrete-state model, a birth–death–immigration process, the statistics are available in closed form. Mean, variance and the index of dispersion all blow up as one over one minus R-naught. The coefficient of variation doesn’t depend on R-naught at all, which is why it turns out to be a poor indicator in practice. So the theory tells us which statistics to compute and also which ones to ignore.

[~1 min]

In simulation, variance and autocorrelation rise before herd immunity is lost

In a city of 8.5 million that starts 92% protected, vaccination declines (blue) and Re rises (red); rolling autocorrelation and variance climb before the outbreak. Drake, Rohani & Winter, Future Microbiol 2026.

Here it is in a simulation sized like New York City. The system starts at measles elimination: 92 percent of the population is protected, there’s no endemic transmission, and the only cases are importations. Then vaccination at birth declines from the dashed line, so herd immunity erodes and the effective reproduction number rises slowly toward one. Incidence stays at a few sporadic cases for years. The bottom panels are rolling autocorrelation and variance computed from the case series alone. Both climb before herd immunity is lost and the outbreak takes off.

That’s the promise. The rest of the talk asks how far it holds up.

[~1.5 min]

Does the signal hold up in surveillance data?

That’s the theory in clean models, and the next thing any epidemiologist will ask is whether it holds up in the data health departments actually collect.

Case reports are aggregated and noisy versions of the true dynamics

True infections (black) differ from perfect weekly reports (blue) and from reports with sampling error (red). Brett et al., PLOS Comput Biol 2018.

Everything so far comes from a simple compartmental model, and that’s the model the theory was derived from. Real epidemics are messier: case reports are incomplete, transmission is seasonal, and populations are structured. So the next question is still a theoretical one. Should we expect critical slowing down to show up under those more realistic conditions? We answer it by simulating the complications and checking whether the indicators still work.

The first complication is observation. Nobody observes the number infected. Health departments see cases, aggregated by week or month and thinned by under-reporting. The black line is the truth, blue is perfect weekly reporting, and red is weekly reporting with realistic sampling error. Postdoctoral associate Toby Brett asked whether we can still see critical slowing down despite this corruption of information.

[~1.5 min]

Most indicators still work under realistic reporting

Seven of ten indicators keep high AUC unless reporting error is highly overdispersed. Brett et al. 2018.

And here are the results of a huge number of simulations, with every combination of reporting probability, reporting overdispersion and aggregation period we could think of. Each row is an indicator and each column a reporting probability, with weekly aggregation on the left and monthly on the right. Blue is good discrimination. Mean, variance and first-differenced variance hold up unless reporting error is highly overdispersed. Autocorrelation needs the aggregation period to be longer than the serial interval. Coefficient of variation, skewness and kurtosis are unreliable, as the closed forms predicted. Seven of ten work under realistic reporting.

[~1.5 min]

Seasonal forcing doesn’t erase critical slowing down

Simulated emergence with no, low and high seasonality, with wavelet power below. Miller et al. 2017.

Another problem is that many vaccine-preventable diseases are strongly seasonal. For childhood infections, school-term forcing is exactly the kind of periodic signal that could swamp a slow trend. Paige Miller simulated emergence with no, low and high seasonal amplitude, left to right. Here’s how to read each column. The top row is R-naught: the black line includes the seasonal forcing, and the blue dashed line is its average, which is flat for ten years and then climbs to one at year fifteen. The dashed gray line marks where that climb begins. The middle row is weekly incidence. The bottom row is the wavelet power spectrum: time runs left to right, period runs down the vertical axis from a few weeks at the top to four years at the bottom, and red means high power. Ignore everything outside the white curve, where edge effects make the spectrum unreliable.

The thing to look for is spectral reddening. As the system slows down, its fluctuations shift toward longer periods, so power moves down the vertical axis. In the non-seasonal panel on the left, the lower right corner turns red after year ten. With strong seasonality, on the right, the annual cycle puts a band of power right at one year, and that band is what makes the problem hard.

[~2 min]

Wavelet filtering stays reliable as seasonality grows

AUC of the top indicators against seasonal amplitude β₁/β₀, under three kinds of preprocessing. Miller et al., Theor Biol Med Model 2017, Fig. 3.

So how well does it work? Each panel plots AUC against seasonal amplitude, from none on the left of each panel to R-naught swinging all the way down to zero and back on the right. Variance, variance convexity and wavelet filtering, which sums wavelet power in a band from about 30 to 115 weeks, stay around 0.8 even at the highest seasonality, whatever preprocessing we use. Decay time, in pink, degrades. The other wavelet statistic, spectral reddening itself, did well without seasonality but fell from about 0.7 to 0.35 as seasonality increased, which is why it isn’t on this plot. The lesson is that seasonality has to be handled on purpose, and a band-filtered wavelet statistic is one good way to do it.

[~1 min]

Early warning signals appear even in models too complex to analyze

Lag-one autocorrelation rises before Reff = 1 in six models, from a birth–death process to the FRED agent-based simulation. Brett et al., PLOS Comput Biol 2020.

Last robustness check, and the most general one. The theory is derived for low-dimensional compartmental models, and real populations are high-dimensional. What we found is that the early warning signals appear even in extremely complex models where mathematical analysis is impossible. Toby Brett ran the same experiment across six models of increasing realism, up to FRED, an agent-based simulation of a real US population with households, schools and workplaces. Here it’s illustrated by lag-one autocorrelation, which rises toward one before the threshold in every model.

[~1 min]

Where it has worked

The signal holds up in simulation, so now let’s look at real outbreaks where it showed up ahead of time.

Malaria in Kenya’s western highlands resurged after years of control

Monthly malaria cases at a tea estate in Kericho, Kenya, 1965–2002. Harris, Hay & Drake, Biol Lett 2020.

The first empirical case is not a vaccine-preventable disease, but it is a resurgence. Malaria at a tea estate in Kericho was held at low levels for decades and came back in the late 1980s and 1990s, plausibly driven by chloroquine resistance and warming. Both are slow drivers.

[~1 min]

Four indicators warned of Kericho’s resurgence two years ahead

Four indicators became significant about 24 months before the transition. Harris, Hay & Drake 2020.

Mallory Harris computed each indicator in a rolling window and assessed significance against 10,000 permutations of the series. Variance, autocovariance, autocorrelation and decay time crossed significance about two years before the transition. That’s the cleanest demonstration I know of in a human epidemic.

[~1 min]

Variance rose before the 1983 monkeypox epidemic in Central and West Africa

Statistically significant increases in variance appear in the bifurcation window. Unpublished analysis.

A second resurgence, this time of a pathogen held in check by a vaccine against a different virus. Smallpox vaccination protects against monkeypox, and when smallpox vaccination stopped after eradication, that cross-protection began to wane. Monkeypox cases in Central and West Africa rose through the early 1980s to an epidemic peak in December 1983. The gray bars are monthly cases and the points are rolling variance, with red and blue marking statistically significant increases. Variance rose through the window before the peak.

One caution before you believe this too much. Surveillance for monkeypox was intensified in the 1980s to verify smallpox eradication, with more than 20,000 specimens tested, so some of that rise may be reporting rather than transmission. This analysis is unpublished.

[~1.5 min]

Signals have also appeared before mumps and pertussis resurgence

  • Mumps, England: a combined decision function rose about four years before the 2004–05 outbreak
  • Pertussis, US states: resurgent states were distinguishable from non-resurgent ones by the early 1990s
  • Measles, Niger: variance and autocorrelation rose before re-emergence and elimination in model-constrained simulations

Brett & Rohani, PLOS Biol 2020; Tredennick et al., J R Soc Interface 2022.

For vaccine-preventable diseases, the best evidence comes from Toby Brett and Pej. They trained a decision function that combines several indicators and applied it to historical case reports. For mumps in England, it signaled rising risk about four years before the 2004–05 outbreak. For pertussis in the US, it separated resurgent states from the rest by the early 1990s, nearly a decade before the national peak. Andrew Tredennick found the classical indicators rising before measles re-emergence and elimination in Niger, though in model-constrained simulations rather than in the raw series. Note the qualifier on each one. None of them is a single indicator rising in raw surveillance data before an outbreak.

[~1 min]

For COVID-19, overlapping timescales scrambled the signal

Indicator trends before Europe’s second COVID-19 wave point both ways across countries. Dablander et al. 2022.

A cautionary case. Fabian Dablander looked for early warning before the second COVID wave in Europe. Each dot is a country and the horizontal axis is the trend in an indicator. They go both ways. The problem was timescales. R changed on the same timescale as the epidemic because of policy and behavior, so there was no slow driver, and the theory doesn’t apply.

[~1.5 min]

US measles, 2006–2025

That brings me back to the question I opened with: could we have seen the US measles outbreaks coming?

We tested whether variance and autocorrelation rose before three major outbreaks

  • Weekly cases, 2006–2025, from a curated domestic dataset plus CDC NNDSS
  • Rolling-window variance and lag-one autocorrelation (our open-source R package)
  • Trend tests with Spearman’s ρ adjusted for autocorrelation
  • Sensitivity grid: 3 detrending × 3 window bandwidths

Drake & Rohani, Future Microbiol 2026.

So, back to the opening question. Pej and I assembled a weekly series of domestic measles cases from 2006 through July 2025, a curated dataset from Shaun Truelove extended with CDC surveillance. We computed variance and lag-one autocorrelation in rolling windows with an R package we built and made publicly available, tested for trends with a Spearman correlation adjusted for autocorrelation, and ran a three-by-three grid of bandwidths so that the result wouldn’t hinge on one tuning choice.

[~1 min]

Neither indicator rose consistently before any of the three outbreaks

Autocorrelation showed a weak positive trend over the full series, significant only under some detrending choices. Variance showed none.

In the windows before 2014, 2018–19 and 2025, neither increased consistently.

Apparent signals appeared only with very large bandwidths and weren’t robust. Drake & Rohani, Future Microbiol 2026.

Here’s the answer to my opening question: no. Over the whole series, autocorrelation had a weak upward trend that reached marginal significance only under some detrending choices. Variance had nothing. In the windows before each of the three outbreaks, neither indicator increased consistently. We could manufacture a signal with very large bandwidths, but it disappeared when we changed them, which is what over-smoothing looks like.

[~1.5 min]

Outbreaks didn’t get larger or longer on the way to 2025

Outbreak duration (r = 0.44, p = 0.18) and size (r = 0.47, p = 0.16) show no trend with start date. Drake & Rohani, Future Microbiol 2026.

We also looked for indirect evidence. If the system were drifting toward the threshold, chains of transmission should get longer, so outbreaks should get bigger and last longer. Each point is an outbreak. There’s no significant trend in duration or size.

[~1 min]

Fade-outs stayed as frequent as ever

The number of weeks with zero, fewer than two, or fewer than six cases in a trailing 52-week window shows no secular decline. Drake & Rohani, Future Microbiol 2026.

Near the threshold, transmission should fade out less often. The count of zero-case and low-case weeks in a trailing year dips during each outbreak and then recovers. There’s no downward drift. Measles in the US kept fading out.

[~1 min]

Four explanations for the missing signal

  1. Direction. Approach from below is intrinsically harder to detect.
  2. Hovering, not drifting. The US may sit in a weakly subcritical regime.
  3. Response. Outbreak control truncates the dynamics the theory describes.
  4. Aggregation. National totals average over asynchronous local dynamics.

Why? I see four explanations, and they aren’t exclusive. First, direction. Suzanne O’Regan and I showed that critical slowing down is intrinsically harder to detect when transmission approaches the threshold from below, as in resurgence, than from above, as in elimination. Second, the US may not be drifting toward the threshold at all. It may be hovering just below it, where importations plus the episodic buildup of susceptibles produce outbreaks like these without any bifurcation. Third, response. Every outbreak triggers case investigation, exclusion and vaccination campaigns, so outbreak size and duration reflect us as much as the virus. Fourth, the national series averages over communities whose immunity gaps are very different and whose dynamics aren’t synchronized.

My own bet is on the second and third.

[~1.5 min]

What it will take

If the theory is right and measles still showed nothing, the gap tells us what has to change, and I see four problems to work on.

Outbreak response has to be part of the model

Theory treats outbreak size as intrinsic dynamics, but for measles every outbreak is truncated by case finding, exclusion and vaccination campaigns.

The first open problem follows directly from measles. The theory assumes that outbreaks end because susceptibles are depleted. In the US they end because the health department ends them. We need early warning theory that includes a controller, and that probably means indicators computed on something other than raw case counts, such as time from importation to detection or chain size among contacts.

[~1 min]

We need to tell drifting from hovering

The same outbreak record can come from a system sliding toward R = 1 or one idling just below it.

Second. Early warning theory is built for a slow drift through the threshold. A system idling just below R equals one generates large, irregular outbreaks with no trend in any indicator. Distinguishing the two needs information the case series doesn’t have, above all coverage and serology, which argues for combining statistical indicators with mechanistic models rather than choosing between them.

[~1 min]

Signals will be local before they are national

Immunity gaps are concentrated in schools, counties and communities, so indicators should be computed where transmission happens.

Third. The immunity gaps that fueled 2019 in New York and 2025 in West Texas are local. Averaging them into a national series throws away exactly the heterogeneity that matters. The spatial indicators I showed, spectral similarity and the covariance eigenvalues, are a start. The bottleneck is timely sub-state case data.

[~1 min]

Evidence is still thin, and a warning isn’t a decision

  • Only 16 of 37 studies of resilience indicators for outbreaks used real data
  • Few show an unambiguous rise in routine surveillance before an outbreak
  • Action thresholds must weigh the asymmetric costs of false alarms and misses

Delecroix et al., PLOS Glob Public Health 2023; Drake, Rohani & Winter, Future Microbiol 2026.

Fourth, and most important for a school of public health. A 2023 systematic review found 37 studies applying resilience indicators to outbreaks, and only 16 used real-world data. We have few clean demonstrations, and almost no estimates of specificity, because nobody publishes the cases where nothing happened. Even a validated indicator needs a pipeline: data cleaning, a threshold for action, and a decision rule that weighs a missed measles outbreak against a false alarm. That’s where epidemiologists, not modelers, have the comparative advantage.

[~1.5 min]

The approach to resurgence should be detectable; for US measles, so far, it hasn’t been

The work now is closing the gap between the theory and the way public health surveillance and response actually operate.

Let me come back to where I started. Could we have seen 2014, 2019 or 2025 coming from the national case series? We looked carefully, and the answer is no. The theory is sound, the simulations show the signal holding up against the problems you’d worry about, and it has turned up in malaria, mpox, mumps and pertussis. Measles in the United States is the hard case, and it’s hard for reasons that are fixable: we need local data, and we need to put the response into the model.

Thank you.

The second factorial moment separates rising transmission from rising reporting

The mean rises whether reporting or transmission increases; the second factorial moment rises only with transmission. O’Dea & Drake, Theor Ecol 2019.

Every epidemiologist in this room will have the same worry. If testing improves, cases go up and variance goes up. How do I know it’s transmission? Eamon O’Dea showed that the second factorial moment, normalized by the squared mean, is invariant to changes in reporting probability but moves with transmission. On the left, reporting increases and the moment stays flat. On the right, transmission increases and it rises.

Resurgence is harder to see coming than elimination

Elimination: coverage rising

Emergence: coverage falling

Theoretical indicators from the van Kampen expansion for SIS and SIR models. O’Regan & Drake, Theor Ecol 2013.

One result from the theory matters a great deal for today. The direction of approach matters. On the left, vaccine uptake is rising toward elimination. On the right, transmission is rising toward emergence. The indicators change in both directions, but Suzanne O’Regan showed that detecting critical slowing down is intrinsically harder on the way up, when the system approaches the threshold from below, starting from sporadic cases. There’s simply less signal to work with.

Wavelet filtering ranks with variance; wavelet reddening degrades with seasonality

AUC of each indicator across seasonal amplitude under three kinds of preprocessing. Miller et al., Theor Biol Med Model 2017.

Across preprocessing choices, variance, autocovariance and wavelet filtering stay near the top. Wavelet spectral reddening sits in the middle of the pack without seasonality and drops toward chance as seasonal amplitude grows. Skewness, kurtosis and the coefficient of variation are poor throughout.

Pertussis spectra across US states converged as vaccination pushed toward the threshold

Multivariate spectral similarity of state-level pertussis series, 1938–1980. Ghadami et al., Nonlinear Dyn 2023.

Space offers another route. Amin Ghadami and Bogdan Epureanu developed an indicator that measures how similar the spectra of fluctuations are across connected locations. Applied to pertussis in US states from 1938 to 1980, spectral similarity rose as vaccination pushed transmission toward the threshold. Spatial coupling can strengthen a signal that single series miss.
